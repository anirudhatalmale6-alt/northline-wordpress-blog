There is a particular kind of software fatigue that comes not from having too few tools but from having too many that almost fit. Six note apps, four task managers, three of them syncing to each other badly. The cure, at least for me, was not finding the one perfect application. It was building a small workbench of things I actually understand.

A workbench is not a toolkit

A toolkit is a collection. A workbench is a collection arranged around the work. The distinction sounds precious until you notice how much of your day is spent moving between tools rather than using them — exporting, re-formatting, remembering which of two places you put the thing.

So the first question is not "what is the best app for X" but "what does the material actually flow through?" For me the answer turned out to be embarrassingly plain: text files, a folder, and a handful of scripts that do one thing each.

The four pieces

Four things do about ninety per cent of the work. None of them are impressive on their own.

  1. A capture key. One global shortcut that appends a timestamped line to a single file. No categories, no tags, no decision at the moment of capture. Decisions at capture time are what kill capture habits.
  2. A daily file. Created automatically each morning, pre-seeded with yesterday's unfinished lines. It is a to-do list that cannot become a backlog, because anything I fail to carry forward three days in a row I clearly did not mean.
  3. A search command. Not a fuzzy semantic thing — a literal, fast, case-insensitive search across the whole folder, bound to two keystrokes. Recall beats organisation.
  4. An archive script. Run monthly. Moves anything untouched for sixty days into a dated folder. Nothing is deleted; nothing stays in the way.

Every tool you add has a maintenance cost you will pay quietly, forever. Sharpen four things rather than buying a fifth.

Sharpening, specifically

Sharpening means shaving friction off a tool you already use, and it is nearly always about the first two seconds. If capture takes four seconds instead of one, I will not capture in a meeting. If search takes a mouse, I will not search mid-sentence. The absolute time is trivial; the threshold effect is not.

A concrete example. My search command originally printed matching lines. Useful, but I kept having to open the file afterwards to get context. One flag later it prints two lines either side and the filename as a clickable path. That change took ninety seconds to make and has probably saved me an hour a month since.

Keep a friction log

The hard part is noticing the friction at all — you adapt to bad tools the way you adapt to a squeaky door. So I keep a file called friction.txt and add a line whenever something annoys me twice. Once a month I read it and fix the top two. Most entries are one-line fixes. A few turn out to be the same problem wearing different hats, which is the most valuable thing the log produces.

What I deliberately do not automate

Reviewing. Deciding. Anything where the friction is the point. I tried automating my weekly review into a generated summary and it was worse than useless — the summary was accurate and I retained none of it, because writing the review was how I was actually thinking about the week. The automation removed the work I wanted to be doing and left the work I did not.

That is the test I now apply before automating anything: is this step transporting material, or is it thinking? Transport should be free. Thinking should cost exactly what it costs.
