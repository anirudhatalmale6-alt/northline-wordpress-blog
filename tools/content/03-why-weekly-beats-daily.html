I kept a daily writing streak for four hundred and eleven days. It produced very little I would show anyone. I have now been publishing weekly for a bit under two years, and roughly half of it is work I still stand behind. The daily habit was better at making me write. The weekly one was better at making me finish.

Daily optimises for the wrong unit

A daily cadence rewards the smallest thing that counts as done today. That is genuinely valuable when the problem is starting — and starting was my problem for years, so the streak was not wasted. But the moment starting stops being the bottleneck, a daily target starts working against you, because almost nothing worth saying can be thought through in one sitting.

The tell is what happens on a bad day. Under a daily target, a bad day produces filler, because filler protects the streak. Under a weekly one, a bad day produces notes, because there are six more days to convert them.

A week has slack in it

This is the real mechanism, and it is not about motivation at all. It is about buffer. A daily commitment has zero slack: any disruption is a failure. A weekly commitment absorbs an illness, a deadline, a bad night's sleep, and still lands.

  • Two strong sessions and five weak ones still make a week.
  • An idea can sit overnight — which is where most of my actual thinking happens.
  • You can throw away Tuesday's work on Wednesday without the whole system collapsing.

Consistency is not the same as frequency. What you want is a rhythm you will still be keeping in a year, not the tightest one you can survive this month.

The shape of my week

It settled into something fairly boring, which I take as a good sign.

  1. Thursday — publish, then immediately pick next week's question. Picking while still warm is much easier than picking cold on Monday.
  2. Friday to Sunday — no writing, just reading and notes. Anything I collect goes in one file with no structure.
  3. Monday — the spine. One line per claim, out loud, ruthless.
  4. Tuesday — expansion. This is the long day, and the only one that needs a real block of time.
  5. Wednesday — revision and cuts. If it is not working by Wednesday evening, it moves to next week rather than getting rescued.

The escape hatch matters

That last point is the thing I would keep if I had to throw away everything else. Having an explicit, non-shameful way to postpone means I never publish something I do not believe just to hit a date. I use it about once every seven weeks. Knowing it exists is what stops the cadence from turning into the same trap the daily streak was.

What I would tell someone starting

If you have never finished anything, go daily and go small — the streak is the right medicine for that disease. But watch for the day when hitting the target starts feeling easy and the output starts feeling thin. That is the signal to stretch the interval, not to raise the word count.

And pick the interval that gives you one full night between the thinking and the deciding. For most things, that is a week. For a few, it is a month. It is almost never a day.
