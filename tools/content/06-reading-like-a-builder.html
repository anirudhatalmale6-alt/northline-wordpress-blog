I read a great deal for years and retained almost none of it. Not because I read badly — I highlighted, I took notes, I did all the recommended things. I retained none of it because I was reading to agree, and agreement leaves no trace.

Reading to agree versus reading to build

Reading to agree is the default mode. You move through the text, nodding, occasionally marking a sentence that phrases something well. It feels productive and is nearly frictionless, which should have been the clue — nothing that changes how you think is frictionless.

Reading to build means you are looking for parts. Not quotes — parts. A mechanism you could reuse, a distinction that cuts somewhere you had not thought to cut, a claim specific enough that it could be wrong. You are not asking "is this true?" so much as "what could I make with this?"

The three-column note

What finally worked was a note format with three columns, and a rule that the second and third are mandatory.

  • Claim. What the text asserts, in my own words, short enough to fit on a line.
  • Mechanism. Why it would be true. If I cannot state a mechanism, I have not understood it — I have only memorised its shape.
  • Test. What I would expect to observe if it were true, and what I would expect if it were not.

The third column is where most of my highlights go to die, and that is the point. An enormous proportion of things that read as insight turn out to predict nothing in particular. Being forced to name an observation exposes that immediately.

If a claim survives being asked what it predicts, it is worth keeping. Most do not, and finding that out early is the whole return on the method.

Reading less, on purpose

The obvious objection is that this is slow, and it is — roughly three times slower per page. I now read maybe a third of what I used to. The trade has been overwhelmingly worth it, but I want to be precise about why, because "read slowly, it's better" is the kind of advice that sounds wise and helps nobody.

It is worth it because the retained fraction went from something like two per cent to something like forty. Reading three times slower and retaining twenty times more is not a close call. And the retention is of a useful kind: I can now argue against the ideas I have read, which I could never do from highlights.

What I skim instead

To make the time back, I am much more aggressive about abandoning. I read the first section and the last section, and if I cannot find a single candidate for column two, I stop. This felt rude at first and now feels like basic hygiene. Most books have one mechanism in them and the rest is illustration.

The exception is anything I disagree with strongly, which I now read more carefully rather than less. Those are the ones where I am most likely to be pattern-matching instead of reading, and where the parts are most likely to be unfamiliar shapes.
