A bad idea is cheap. You try it, it fails quickly, you move on. A good idea — a genuinely good one that is nonetheless not the thing you should be doing right now — can cost you a year. It is good enough to keep working, which means it never produces the clean failure that would let you stop.

The shape of the trap

It always looks like progress. Each week produces something real: a feature, a section, a small improvement you can point to. Nobody would call it wasted. And yet at the end of the year the thing has not become what it needed to become, because the constraint was never effort — it was that the idea, though good, was answering a question nobody was asking loudly enough.

I have done this twice. Both times the warning signs were visible in month two, and both times I read them as ordinary difficulty rather than as signal.

Three signs I now take seriously

  1. Explaining it keeps getting longer. Not more precise — longer. When the explanation grows because each version raises an objection the previous one did not, the idea is accumulating caveats rather than clarity.
  2. The people who like it most are the ones least likely to use it. Enthusiasm from adjacent bystanders is pleasant and nearly worthless. Mild interest from someone with the problem is worth more than delight from someone without it.
  3. Every milestone reveals another prerequisite. One or two is normal. A pattern of them means the real work is somewhere you have not looked, and you are building the visible part because it is the part you know how to build.

The dangerous projects are not the ones that are failing. They are the ones that are succeeding slightly, indefinitely.

The kill criterion, written in advance

What actually helped was not better judgement in the moment — my judgement in the moment is compromised by everything I have already invested. It was writing down, at the start, a specific observation that would make me stop.

Specific is the operative word. "If it is not working by June" is useless, because "working" will be redefined by June. "If fewer than five people have used it twice in the same week by June" is a fact about the world that my future self cannot argue with. The whole value is in taking the decision away from the person who will be too invested to make it.

Stopping is not the same as discarding

Both times I stopped, most of the work survived — as components, as things I had learned how to build, once as a much smaller tool that was genuinely useful precisely because it was smaller. The year was not wasted. It was just spent on the wrong scale of the right thing.

That is the version of this I wish someone had told me earlier: the question is rarely "is this idea good?" It is "is this the right size of this idea, now?" Good ideas at the wrong size are the most expensive thing I know of, and they never feel like a mistake while you are making one.
