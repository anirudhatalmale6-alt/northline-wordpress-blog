Three months ago I moved everything out of a private folder and started publishing as I went. Not finished essays — working notes, half-formed arguments, things I was still unsure about. Here is what actually happened, including the parts that did not go the way the advice promised.

The promised benefit was real, but slower

The claim is that publishing early gets you corrections. It does, eventually. In month one I got almost nothing — two replies, both encouraging and neither useful. The correction only started arriving once there was enough material for someone to see a pattern in it, which for me was somewhere around the fifteenth post.

When it did arrive it was worth the wait. The single most useful message I received pointed out that three separate pieces were making the same argument with different vocabulary, and that I had not noticed because I never read them next to each other. That observation reorganised about half of what I thought I believed.

The unpromised cost

Nobody warned me about this one: publishing a half-formed idea makes it harder to abandon. Once it is out there with my name on it, some quiet part of my brain starts defending it. Private notes get discarded freely. Public ones acquire a constituency of one.

The risk of writing in public is not embarrassment. It is premature commitment to a position you only meant to try on.

The countermeasure I landed on is dating everything visibly and adding a short "where I am now" line at the top of anything I have changed my mind about. It sounds like a small formatting choice. It is actually the thing that lets me keep publishing uncertain work, because it makes revising in public normal rather than a retraction.

Numbers, since people ask

  • 31 posts in 92 days, of which 9 I would call finished thinking and the rest working notes.
  • Median time to publish from first note: 4 days. The two pieces I am proudest of took 19 and 26.
  • Replies that changed something: 6. All six came from people who had read more than one post.
  • Posts I have since substantially revised: 5.

The last number is the one I watch. If it were zero I would suspect I was only publishing things I was already certain about, which defeats the purpose.

What I would do differently

I would separate the two things I was doing. "Working notes" and "essays" want different formats, different lengths and, honestly, different expectations from a reader — and mixing them in one stream meant the notes felt underbaked and the essays felt buried. I have since split them into two categories with different templates, and both improved immediately.

I would also stop reading the analytics daily. Not for the usual reason about vanity metrics — because the numbers are simply too noisy at this scale to mean anything, and I kept drawing conclusions from what was mathematically indistinguishable from nothing. Weekly is often enough to see a trend and rare enough not to chase one.

Would I recommend it?

Yes, with one condition: have somewhere genuinely private to be wrong. Writing in public works for me precisely because it is not the only place I write. The private folder still exists, it is still messy, and everything good passes through it first.
